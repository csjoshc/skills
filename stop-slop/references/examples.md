# Before/After Examples

These examples show edits, not templates. Keep a phrase or structure when it carries meaning in the passage.

## Example 1: Throat-clearing and a false contrast

**Before:**
> "Here's the thing: building products is hard. Not because the technology is complex. Because people are complex. Let that sink in."

**After:**
> "Building a product is hard, mostly because people have to agree on what it should do."

The disagreement is the point. The opening and final line only add drama.

## Example 2: Filler and reassurance

**Before:**
> "It turns out that most teams struggle with alignment. The uncomfortable truth is that nobody wants to admit they're confused. And that's okay."

**After:**
> "Most teams struggle to stay aligned. People don't always say when they're confused."

"Most" is a real qualifier, so it stays. The opener and closer add no information.

## Example 3: Jargon and a vague warning

**Before:**
> "In today's fast-paced landscape, we need to lean into discomfort and navigate uncertainty with clarity. This matters because your competition isn't waiting."

**After:**
> "Decide with the info we have. Competitors keep moving."

Action: make the call with current information. The business jargon doesn't help.

## Example 4: Dramatic fragments and a familiar slogan

**Before:**
> "Speed. Quality. Cost. You can only pick two. That's it. That's the tradeoff."

**After:**
> "Speed / quality / cost. Which can move?"

Speed, quality, and cost are distinct. The old version turns them into a fake rule.

## Example 5: Rhetorical setup and a false choice

**Before:**
> "What if I told you that the best teams don't optimize for productivity? Here's what I mean: they optimize for learning. Think about it."

**After:**
> "Ship the work, then check whether it solved the problem."

Teams can track output and learn from the work. The "best teams" claim, setup, and prompt add nothing.
